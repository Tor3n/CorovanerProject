# Exploration and campaign progression

This is collaborator analysis of
[the creator's vision](vision.md). Proposed mechanics below are not approved canon.

## Requirements derived from the vision

The opening is an RPG about one traveling character: following roads, visiting
sites, completing quests, and establishing relationships with people, tribes,
nations, and other factions. Later, the game suddenly becomes a 4X game. The
character manages a caravan empire and may pursue authority as a ruler, caravan
master, faith leader, or another creator-approved form of leadership. Earlier
choices shape the factions and situations the player must then handle.

The exact trigger, stage lengths, combat system, strategic turn structure, and
leadership mechanics have not been decided. No particular catastrophe, succession,
war, or chosen-one story is established as the trigger for the genre change. The
ancient End-day exists in the setting, but its cause remains unchosen.

Caravans and trade carry the main story. The player charts routes through deserts,
scarce forests, industrial wastelands, and great city ruins in a world of small
states that trade, fight, and intermarry. Most people live by agriculture; advanced
factions and sometimes successful deliberately medieval communities coexist.
Rural wagons drawn by oxen or horses share the setting with cars, scarce railways,
electric propulsion, and drones. Hostile communities may fire on strangers, and
developed air defences mean flight cannot simply be assumed to bypass local power.
These are setting requirements; exact transport and economic mechanics remain open.

Semi-sentient and hive-mind robots, diverse religions, and desires for metal body
modification belong in the world. Magic and mutants do not. The opening provides
no reliable account of the End-day, and uncovering its cause is not an established
main objective. See [world.md](world.md) for the source-derived boundaries.

## Proposed exploration loop

Notice a place or hear about it; choose a route; travel and discover; meet people
and make a decision; leave with knowledge, changed relationships, and a new reason
to travel. Repeat with growing understanding of the same country.

Exploration rewards can include information, access, relationships, and recognition
as well as equipment or currency. Routes should present understandable trade-offs.
Empty distance should establish scale and pacing, not only consume time. The
country map must remain an interesting place to play, rather than a decorative
selector between detailed combat scenes.

## Proposed route and trade decisions

The following are candidate ways to make charting a route interesting. They do
not approve a resource list, simulation depth, faction roster, or vehicle system.

| Route consideration | Possible player decision |
| --- | --- |
| Terrain and distance | Compare a direct desert crossing with a longer road through inhabited land or a difficult passage through ruins. |
| Agriculture and markets | Learn where a caravan might obtain food, fodder, or goods, and whether the detour is worthwhile. |
| Political boundaries | Seek local permission, negotiate access, or change course when relations between neighboring states change. |
| Transport and support | Choose a viable transport arrangement for the road and available repairs, power, or supplies; do not assume advanced vehicles are always the best choice. |
| Airspace and hostility | Learn who tolerates drones or unfamiliar traffic before relying on them; avoid treating flight as risk-free fast travel. |
| Beliefs and trust | Learn a community's customs and attitudes toward outsiders, machines, or body modification, with variation between communities. |

Not every journey needs every consideration. The loop should make exploration and
trade understandable, with local people and discoveries giving the decisions
meaning. Any supplies, maintenance, toll, diplomacy, or airspace system requires
its own design before implementation.

## Proposed continuity across the genre change

Keep the same recognizable geography and persistent campaign history. The scope
of the player's responsibility changes suddenly; the meaning of familiar places
changes with it. This supports the surprise without discarding the RPG journey.

| Earlier experience | Possible later consequence | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Help or exploit a settlement during a quest. | Trust affects access, negotiations, assistance, or resistance. | Proposed example. |
| Learn a difficult road and meet its local guides. | Route knowledge and relationships offer a logistical advantage. | Proposed example. |
| Make a promise to a tribe or faction. | That promise becomes a diplomatic obligation at a larger scale. | Proposed example. |
| Respect or undermine a religious community. | Its support or opposition matters if the player seeks spiritual authority. | Proposed example. |
| Earn permission to cross a small state's territory. | Access or obligations affect the later caravan network. | Proposed example. |
| Learn where a route supports animal-drawn or advanced transport. | Practical route knowledge informs later trade decisions. | Proposed example. |

These examples define no named faction, numerical bonus, quest, or mandatory path.
They illustrate how the creator's requirement for consequences could work.

## Proposed handling of the sudden change

Before the reveal, present the journey as a complete and worthwhile RPG. Avoid
locked empire tabs, strategy tutorials, future ruler progress bars, or quest copy
that advertises the coming genre. Relationships and events can make sense locally
without explaining their future strategic use.

At the reveal, deliver a decisive change in available actions and responsibility.
Introduce the new controls and an understandable first problem at that point.
Tutorials may unfold after the sudden shift; the shift itself should not become a
gradual, advertised unlock ladder.

Surprise does not require arbitrary punishment. Let earlier actions have coherent
consequences, explain those consequences when they matter, and provide ways to
respond. Do not require the player to predict an undisclosed strategic system to
avoid an unwinnable campaign. The desired response is surprise followed by a
recognition that their previous journey mattered. Validate this with playtests;
no document can guarantee that every player will fail to guess the twist.

## Proposed interpretation of 4X

| Strategic activity | Candidate expression in Corovaner |
| --- | --- |
| Explore | Discover routes, resources, peoples, and information; deepen knowledge of familiar regions. |
| Expand | Extend caravan reach, agreements, influence, or control through existing geography. |
| Exploit | Organize trade, supplies, labor, and access to resources. |
| Exterminate / overcome rivals | Support conquest where appropriate, alongside diplomacy, integration, alliance, or other creator-approved outcomes. |

The creator explicitly allows different relationships with factions. Literal
elimination is not assumed to be mandatory for every leadership path. Distinct
ruler, caravan-master, and faith-leader routes need their own meaningful decisions;
whether they are exclusive paths, overlapping identities, or outcomes remains open.
Strategy must work with the static-landscape direction; a base-building loop is
not implied by the word 4X.

## Proposed persistent information

Eventually retain stable place/faction identities, visits and discoveries, meaningful
choices and promises, relationships, and the campaign stage. The strategic opening
should be derived from that history rather than replacing it with a generic start.
Exact data structures and migration work belong in implementation tasks after the
mechanics are chosen. Existing saves do not yet imply these systems exist.
